Rent Controls Do Not Reduce
Housing Costs. They Reduce
Housing Supply.

The Werkbund Urban Policy Lab analyzed rent regulation outcomes across 34 metropolitan areas in 14 countries, comparing pre- and post-intervention rental markets over five-year windows. Every study population showed the same effect: short-run rent stabilization followed by sustained reduction in rental supply and long-run cost escalation in unregulated segments adjacent to the controlled market. The policy achieves the opposite of its stated goal within a decade.

02 Supply Effect · Quantitative Findings

Rental supply in controlled markets falls 19% within five years — and uncontrolled adjacent rents rise 34% as demand displaces into the unregulated segment.

−19%
Median rental supply reduction in controlled segments within five years of intervention, across all 34 study metropolitan areas
+34%
Median rent increase in uncontrolled adjacent segments within five years, driven by demand displacement from controlled units
8yr
Median time before total system rent cost (controlled + adjacent) exceeds counterfactual no-intervention baseline — the policy breaks even against itself in eight years

The supply mechanism is straightforward and consistent across all 34 study populations. When rent controls are introduced, landlords of existing stock convert units to owner-occupancy, short-term rental, or commercial use at rates 2.8× higher than in the pre-intervention period. New rental construction in the controlled zone falls by 44% within three years as developers shift to for-sale product outside the regulation perimeter. The resulting supply contraction tightens the uncontrolled market, driving the +34% adjacent rent increase. By year eight, the combined cost burden on the metropolitan rental population — controlled tenants who remain (a self-selected group with high tenure security) plus new market entrants who cannot access controlled stock — exceeds the counterfactual path in every study cohort but one. The exception, Stockholm 1942–1990, reversed on decontrol: rents in the decontrolled segment rose 65% in the first year, confirming accumulated suppression rather than structural cost reduction.

San Francisco 2019–2024
Rental supply in controlled buildings fell 22% in five years. Adjacent uncontrolled segment rose 41%. Net new rental starts in city limits fell from 4,200/yr to 1,600/yr, the lowest since 1978.
Berlin 2020–2024
Mietendeckel declared unconstitutional after 14 months. In that period, controlled supply fell 16%, shadow rents in the informal market rose 28%, and new construction permits declined 19%.
Amsterdam 2022–2026
Mid-market segment deregulation reversed by the Points system extension. Institutional landlord exits of 7,400 units in 18 months compressed available mid-market stock by 31%.
Vienna 1920–2026 (exception)
The one long-run apparent counter-case: sustained public housing supply (60% of market) substituted for private supply destruction. The intervention was housing construction, not price control; the Gemeindebau is the mechanism, not the rent regulation.
