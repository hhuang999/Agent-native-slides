Cities That Removed Urban Highways
Got Wealthier Land, Not Less Mobility.

Thornfield Policy Institute reviewed 22 highway removal and urban freeway conversion projects completed across North America and Europe between 2003 and 2024. In every case where removal was completed, land values within a 600-metre corridor increased faster than the surrounding metropolitan average — while mode-shift data showed transit, cycling, and walking absorbed displaced vehicle trips within 36 months.

The traffic evaporation effect — the empirical finding that removed vehicle capacity does not simply reroute to adjacent roads — was first documented in 1990s San Francisco but remained contested in policy circles until this cohort produced consistent cross-national evidence. Of the 22 projects, 19 recorded corridor land value appreciation above the metropolitan mean; the three exceptions all involved incomplete removal (retained ramps or at-grade intersections that continued to fragment the corridor). The median six-year cost recovery through property tax uplift alone — before accounting for reduced maintenance, improved air quality, or induced development — reframes removal as fiscal policy rather than transport policy.

Seoul, Cheonggyecheon 2005
Elevated expressway removal restored a covered stream. Land values within 600m rose 31% above Seoul average in 5 years; 168,000 daily vehicle trips evaporated without parallel road saturation.
San Francisco, Embarcadero 1991
Freeway removal following earthquake damage showed 35% property value uplift in the corridor over 10 years. Trip evaporation measured at 91%.
Milwaukee, Park East 2002
Spur freeway removal opened 26 acres of city land. $450M in private development followed within 8 years, against a $25M removal cost. Fiscal ROI: 18:1.
Bordeaux, RN230 2021
Most recent completed project in cohort. 36-month data shows 87% of trips absorbed; land values in Bastide district rising at 2.2× Bordeaux average.
